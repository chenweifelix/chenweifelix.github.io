<section id="overview" class="research-section research-lede" aria-labelledby="overview-heading">
  <p class="eyebrow">Research overview</p>
  <h2 id="overview-heading">Emotion is personal, relational, and public.</h2>
  <p class="research-lede__text">My research asks how people’s beliefs and motives about emotion guide what they feel and do—and how those processes accumulate into consequences for well-being, relationships, and collective life. I study emotion across levels of analysis, from individual regulation choices to conversations, online discourse, and the use of psychological evidence in policy.</p>
</section>

<section id="well-being" class="research-section" aria-labelledby="well-being-heading">
  <p class="research-section__number" aria-hidden="true">01</p>
  <h2 id="well-being-heading">Emotion regulation and well-being</h2>
  <p>I investigate why people regulate emotion and whether their beliefs, goals, and habitual strategies help them build satisfying lives. This work examines both brief emotional episodes and outcomes that unfold over months or years.</p>
  <p>Current questions include how beliefs about the desirability and controllability of emotion shape regulation; when expressive suppression carries costs; and whether emotion-regulation patterns predict stable, long-term well-being. I am also interested in emotion’s role in goal pursuit—for example, how people manage the affective pull of temptations while working toward longer-term aims.</p>
</section>

<section id="relationships" class="research-section" aria-labelledby="relationships-heading">
  <p class="research-section__number" aria-hidden="true">02</p>
  <h2 id="relationships-heading">Interpersonal emotion and relationships</h2>
  <p>Emotion regulation rarely happens in isolation. I study how people express, interpret, and respond to emotion in conversation, and how these dynamics can support—or strain—close relationships and shared goals.</p>
  <p>This line of work connects self-report measures with behavioral traces of interaction. One project uses language similarity to ask when conversational alignment accompanies better emotional experiences, while related work examines how beliefs about social emotion expression shape what people choose to share and how satisfied they feel afterward.</p>
</section>

<section id="society" class="research-section" aria-labelledby="society-heading">
  <p class="research-section__number" aria-hidden="true">03</p>
  <h2 id="society-heading">Emotion in public discourse, society, and policy</h2>
  <p>I extend questions about emotion and motivation beyond the individual and the dyad. In political discussion, I examine why people express outrage online, what observers infer from that expression, and how those inferences may shape social and political consequences.</p>
  <p>I am also interested in how psychological knowledge travels through institutions. Ongoing work traces citations to psychology research in United States policy documents, connecting behavioral science to the public decisions it may inform.</p>
</section>

<section id="methods" class="research-section" aria-labelledby="methods-heading">

  <h2 id="methods-heading">Approach and methods</h2>
  <p>I use quantitative survey methods, longitudinal designs, experiments, and computational analyses of language and behavioral data. My work combines confirmatory tests with exploratory modeling, and I value both predictive performance and explanations that remain legible to theory.</p>
